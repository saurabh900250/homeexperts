Common HVAC Symptoms — What They Usually Mean

General guidance only — a licensed technician needs to inspect your specific system to confirm the cause. This is not a substitute for a professional diagnosis.

Warm Air From AC Vents

Often a refrigerant issue, a failing compressor, or a tripped breaker. Check your thermostat setting and air filter first, but if warm air continues, it usually needs a technician.

Same-day recommended

Furnace Blowing Cold Air

Common causes include a dirty flame sensor, a failed ignitor, or gas supply interruption. Some furnaces run a brief cold-air cycle before heating — if it persists, call for a check.

Same-day recommended

System Cycles On/Off Frequently

Known as short-cycling — often linked to an oversized unit, low refrigerant, a dirty filter, or thermostat placement issues. Left unaddressed, it adds wear and raises bills.

Worth a checkup

Unusual Grinding or Screeching

Usually a motor bearing, worn belt, or loose component. These sounds tend to get worse, not better, and are best addressed before a full breakdown.

Same-day recommended

Water Pooling Near Indoor Unit

Typically a clogged condensate drain line or a frozen evaporator coil that has started to thaw. Left alone it can cause water damage — worth prompt attention.

Same-day recommended

Rising Energy Bills, Same Usage

Points to declining system efficiency — dirty coils, aging components, or duct leaks are common culprits. A tune-up or diagnostic can usually identify the source.

Worth a checkup

Musty or Burning Smell

A musty smell often means mold in ductwork or the drain pan. A burning smell can indicate an electrical issue and should be treated as urgent — shut the system off and call.

Call immediately

System Is 12+ Years Old

Most HVAC systems are built to last 12–20 years depending on maintenance and usage. If yours is approaching that range, a maintenance check helps you plan ahead rather than react to a failure.

Routine check

Weak or Uneven Airflow

Weak airflow at the vents often points to a clogged filter, blocked return, failing blower motor, or leaky ductwork. Uneven airflow room-to-room can mean duct sizing or balancing issues.

Worth a checkup

Thermostat Not Responding

Could be a dead battery, wiring issue, or a thermostat that's failed and needs replacing. If the display is blank or unresponsive, check the breaker before assuming the worst.

Routine check

Ice Forming on Outdoor or Indoor Unit

Ice buildup usually means restricted airflow or low refrigerant. Running the system with ice on it can damage the compressor — switch to fan-only mode and call for service.

Same-day recommended

High Humidity Indoors

An oversized system, low refrigerant, or a failing component can leave a home feeling sticky even when the temperature reads correctly. A technician can check sizing and charge.

Worth a checkup

Frequent Circuit Breaker Trips

A breaker that trips repeatedly when the HVAC system starts can indicate a failing capacitor, compressor strain, or an electrical fault. Treat this as urgent — don't keep resetting it.

Call immediately

System Won't Turn On At All

Check the thermostat batteries, the breaker panel, and the furnace/AC power switch first. If those check out, it's typically an electrical or control-board issue needing a technician.

Same-day recommended
Know Your Equipment

Types of HVAC Systems, Explained

Not sure what you have or what fits your home? Here's a plain-language breakdown of the most common system types.

Central Air Conditioning

Uses an outdoor condenser/compressor and an indoor evaporator coil connected to ductwork, cooling the whole home from a single system.

Best for: homes with existing ductwork wanting whole-home cooling.

Gas or Electric Furnace

Heats air using a gas burner or electric element, then pushes it through ductwork with a blower fan. Most common heating system in colder climates.

Best for: homes needing dedicated, powerful winter heating.

Heat Pump

Moves heat rather than generating it — cools in summer and reverses to heat in winter, using electricity year-round. Efficient in moderate climates.

Best for: homes wanting one system for both heating and cooling.

Ductless Mini-Split

Indoor wall or ceiling units connected to an outdoor compressor with no ductwork needed. Each zone can be controlled independently.

Best for: additions, converted spaces, or homes without ducts.

Packaged Unit

Combines heating and cooling components into a single outdoor cabinet, often installed on a roof or slab — common where indoor space is limited.

Best for: smaller homes, mobile homes, or limited indoor mechanical space.

Zoned HVAC System

Uses dampers in the ductwork and multiple thermostats to heat or cool specific areas of a home independently rather than the whole house at once.

Best for: multi-story homes or homes with hot/cold spot complaints.

Understanding Ratings

SEER, AFUE & Efficiency Ratings — What They Mean

Efficiency ratings tell you how much cooling or heating you get per unit of energy used. Higher generally means lower operating cost, but higher upfront price.

SEER (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio) rates air conditioners and heat pumps. It measures cooling output over a typical season divided by energy used — higher SEER means more cooling per dollar of electricity.

AFUE (Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency) rates furnaces as a percentage — an 95% AFUE furnace turns 95% of its fuel into heat, with the rest lost as exhaust.

HSPF (Heating Seasonal Performance Factor) rates the heating side of heat pumps, similar in concept to SEER but for winter performance.

A technician can tell you your current system's rating and whether upgrading makes sense for your specific energy usage and climate.

13–14

Minimum SEER typically required for new systems in most regions — entry-level efficiency.

15–17

Mid-range efficiency — noticeable savings over older 10–12 SEER systems.

18–22+

High-efficiency systems — higher upfront cost, lower long-term operating cost.

80% AFUE

Standard-efficiency furnace — common in older installations.

95%+ AFUE

High-efficiency condensing furnace — less fuel wasted as exhaust heat.

Beyond Temperature

Indoor Air Quality Solutions

Your HVAC system does more than heat and cool — it circulates the air your household breathes. These add-ons are commonly discussed during a service visit.

Whole-Home Air Filtration

Upgraded filters or media cabinets that trap finer particles than a standard filter.

UV Air Purification

UV lamps installed near the coil to reduce mold and microbial growth in the system.

Humidity Control

Whole-home humidifiers or dehumidifiers to keep comfort levels consistent year-round.

Fresh-Air Ventilation

ERV/HRV systems that bring in filtered outdoor air without losing conditioned indoor air.

Controls

Thermostat Options

The right thermostat can make a real difference in comfort and energy use — here's how the main categories compare.

Manual / Non-Programmable

Set-it-yourself dial or digital display with no scheduling. Simple and reliable, but requires manual adjustment for savings.

Programmable

Lets you set different temperatures for different times of day automatically — a common upgrade for household energy savings.

Smart / Wi-Fi Connected

Controlled from a phone app, often with learning algorithms, remote diagnostics, and usage reports. Some integrate with smart home systems.

Understanding The Estimate

What Affects HVAC Repair & Replacement Cost

Every home and system is different. These are the factors a technician typically weighs when building your written estimate.

01

Type of System

Central AC, heat pump, furnace, ductless mini-split, and packaged units all have different parts and labor requirements.

02

Age & Brand

Older or discontinued models can mean harder-to-source parts, which affects both cost and turnaround time.

03

Severity of the Issue

A capacitor or contactor swap is quick and low-cost. A compressor or heat exchanger issue involves more labor and parts cost.

04

Refrigerant Type

Older systems using R-22 refrigerant typically cost more to service since it's being phased out and is harder to source.

05

Ductwork Condition

Leaky, undersized, or damaged ducts can add to the scope of work needed to fix the root cause, not just the symptom.

06

Home Size & Layout

Square footage, ceiling height, and system accessibility (attic, crawlspace, rooftop) all factor into labor time.

Plan Ahead

Seasonal HVAC Checklist

A little prevention goes a long way. Here's what a pre-season tune-up typically covers.

Before Summer: AC Readiness

  • Check refrigerant charge and pressure
  • Clean condenser and evaporator coils
  • Inspect capacitor, contactor, and wiring
  • Replace or clean air filters
  • Test thermostat calibration and airflow

Before Winter: Heating Readiness

  • Inspect heat exchanger for cracks
  • Test ignitor, flame sensor, and burners
  • Check carbon monoxide safety controls
  • Verify gas pressure and connections
  • Replace filters and check ductwork seals

Do Your Due Diligence

How To Choose An HVAC Technician

Whoever you call — us or anyone else — these are the questions worth asking before you approve any work.

Are they licensed and insured in your state, and can they show proof?

Do they give a written estimate before starting any work, not just a verbal quote?

Do they explain the diagnosis in plain language, not just tell you what it costs?

Do they offer a warranty on parts and labor for the work performed?

Are they upfront about trip or diagnostic fees before they arrive?

Do they push a full replacement immediately without exploring repair options first?

Warranties: What To Expect

Most HVAC repairs and installations carry two separate warranties worth asking about: a manufacturer's warranty covering equipment parts (terms vary by brand and registration), and a labor warranty from the servicing technician covering their workmanship. Ask your technician to confirm both in writing as part of your estimate — coverage terms are set by the manufacturer and the individual technician, not by Home Experts.

Speak The Language

HVAC Glossary — Common Terms Explained

BTU
British Thermal Unit — measures heating/cooling capacity. Higher BTU generally means more heating or cooling power.
TON (of cooling)
A measure of AC capacity equal to 12,000 BTU/hour — most homes use systems between 1.5 and 5 tons.
SEER / SEER2
Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio — the cooling efficiency rating for AC units and heat pumps.
AFUE
Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency — the heating efficiency rating for furnaces, shown as a percentage.
Refrigerant
The chemical compound that absorbs and releases heat as it cycles through the system — common types include R-410A and the phased-out R-22.
Condenser
The outdoor unit that releases heat absorbed from inside the home (in AC mode).
Evaporator Coil
The indoor coil that absorbs heat from air passing over it, allowing the air to cool.
Capacitor
An electrical component that helps start and run the compressor and fan motors — a common repair item.
Heat Exchanger
The furnace component that transfers heat from combustion gases to the air circulated through your home.
Ductwork
The system of channels that distributes heated or cooled air throughout the home.
Short-Cycling
When a system turns on and off more frequently than normal, often due to sizing or component issues.
Zoning
Dividing a home into separate temperature-controlled areas using dampers and multiple thermostats.

While You Wait For A Technician

If you smell gas, hear a hissing sound, or your carbon monoxide detector goes off, leave the house immediately and call your gas utility or 911 before calling for HVAC service. For non-emergency issues, a few things you can safely check yourself:

  • Check your thermostat is set to the correct mode and temperature.
  • Check your air filter — a clogged filter is one of the most common causes of poor airflow.
  • Check your breaker panel for a tripped breaker connected to the HVAC system.
  • Clear debris or vegetation from around an outdoor condenser unit.
